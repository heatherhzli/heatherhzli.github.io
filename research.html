Research

My work centres on what I call environmental hierarchy: the idea that the international system ranks not only people, but also the non-human world. I’m especially interested in how international law takes in Indigenous understandings of land, and what gets lost when land as a living relationship is turned into a resource.

MSc thesis

Ecocide as Event, Ecocide as Condition: Environmental Death and the Constitution of International Order (1970–1998)

LSE, 2026. Supervised by Dr Shikha Dilawri.

My thesis asks a simple question: what did people have to believe the environment was in order to say it had been killed? To answer it, I follow the word “ecocide” from 1970, when it was coined to describe the American use of Agent Orange in Vietnam, to the drafting of the Rome Statute in 1998.

The official story treats environmental death as an event: sudden, visible, and clearly separate from normal life. But Indigenous and anti-colonial writers, such as Ken Saro-Wiwa, described something slower, a destruction built into ordinary development. I argue that the international order’s real power lies less in killing environments than in deciding which of those deaths count. I call this environmental necropolitics. It is why the destruction of Vietnam’s forests was called ecocide, while the destruction of Ogoni land in Nigeria was called development.

BA thesis

Global environmental hierarchy in international environmental agreements

University of Oxford, 2025. Supervised by Professor Louise Fawcett and Weishen Zeng.

My undergraduate thesis was where the idea of environmental hierarchy began. Using archival material and critical discourse analysis, I looked at how international environmental agreements rank and value different parts of the natural world.

How I work

Most of my work is archival and qualitative: discourse analysis of legal documents and historical records, coding texts against shared frameworks, and interviews. I also work in R for data analysis and visualisation.
