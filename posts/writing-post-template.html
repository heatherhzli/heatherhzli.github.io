Working Paper

Incorporative Violence and the Genealogy of Ecocide

This paper traces how Indigenous relational ontologies have been appropriated and transformed through the codification of ecocide in international criminal law. It develops the concept of incorporative violence — a mode of epistemic harm in which non-Western knowledge systems are absorbed into international legal frameworks in ways that neutralise their critical and relational content.

The Problem

The growing international momentum behind ecocide — most visibly through the ICC-ASP/23/26 proposal submitted by Vanuatu, Fiji, and Samoa — has been widely celebrated as a step toward environmental justice. Yet the genealogy of ecocide as a legal concept reveals a more complex story: one in which Indigenous relationships to land are translated into a rights-based vocabulary that systematically strips them of their relational, reciprocal, and place-specific character.

The paper argues that this translation is not incidental but structural — a recurring pattern in which the international legal order extends its jurisdiction by incorporating the normative content of non-Western traditions while foreclosing the epistemological premises on which they rest.

Method

The analysis combines Foucauldian genealogy with critical discourse analysis, drawing on Fairclough and Wodak's attention to silences and absences in institutional texts. Primary sources include:

  • The ICC-ASP/23/26 proposal and associated preparatory documents
  • The Independent Expert Panel's 2021 draft definition of ecocide
  • Submissions from Indigenous legal scholars and organisations during the consultation process
  • Historical precedents in the genealogy of "environmental security" discourse

Key Arguments

The paper develops three core claims. First, the legal codification of ecocide performs a specific kind of epistemic work: it converts relational ontologies into individual rights claims, rendering them legible to international criminal law while severing their connection to place, community, and reciprocity. Second, this conversion is not neutral but productive — it generates new legal subjects and jurisdictional claims that extend the reach of the international criminal order. Third, attending to these dynamics requires not abandoning the ecocide project but approaching it with what the paper calls genealogical vigilance: a sustained critical attention to the epistemic costs of legal translation.

The question is not whether ecocide should be codified, but whether the act of codification can be made accountable to the knowledge systems it claims to protect.
